How To Read Better

Thu Oct 29 2026 19:00:00 GMT-0500 (Central Daylight Time)

How to read a book, the classic guide to intelligent reading - Mortimer J. Adler and Charles Van Doren

One of the greatest skills you can learn as a reader, is understanding how to read better. Many discuss illiteracy rates, school system failures, or some even point to the mild decline of cognitive abilities over time (Or, as I like to call it, the reverse flynn effect).

While I won't discuss data and statistics, what I will say is that just understanding the basics of reading is not enough. One must know how to intelligently read, and not mere consume.

How I discovered Adlers work

In the beginning of 2026, I was looking for a new read. I was graciously recommended a wonderful book by my YouTube algorithm titled Cyclonopedia: Complicity with anonymous materials In which, I have just written about.

It wasn't the typical 'classic', or the lliad by homer, but a theory fiction book.

The premise of the book interested me, but as I beginned reading on chapter 1, I could not understand a single word from that point on.

This book had lots of terms made by the author, it required that I had some knowledge on iranian history, persia history, and demanded that I paid lots of attention and paid close attention to the premises established.

Yet, I wanted to keep reading.

However, one thing was very clear, I could not read this book in the sameway that I have read any other book in the past. I had to actually be very active in the conversation and story.

It was also the point where I realized I needed a system or guidance of some sort of how to actually stay active in reading.

So, I digged through the rubbish in my closet and behold, I had a copy of adler's book the entire time! Not sure why it was in there..

I've always had a copy of this book, but it never occurred to me to why exactly I need to apparently learn how to read all over again. When I opened the table of contents to get a brief idea of what the book was going to go in-depth on, I realized that this book applied specifically to me.

I devoured that book like black-tar heroin, and was one of the few books I managed to read from front to back in a couple years.

The key lession I learned was that although reading good books was indeed difficult, it wasn't difficult in the traditional sense of the word.

It didn't require some innate cognitive traits, it required a more step-by-step approach. Applying this systematic angle to the book has helped me not only to understand the actual text, but to the point where I was able to become truly equal with the author. That is to say, that I knew what he was talking about, and I as a reader could finally talk back to him and actually argue on some points.

Why to read better

I think the question that most are obsessed with these days during the mid`st of constant stimulation all around us is: "How can I read more?"

I too had a similar question. Unlike most however, I didn't really grow having an interest in reading. When I became much older, I realized that there was an entire world on the other side that for some reason I just didn't explore.

I think one of the reasons for that was because I viewed a book as a boring inanimate one way communication object. I think even most people that do enjoy the act of reading view it in a similar fashion as well.

That may as well be one of the prominant reasons to why books are consumed passively, almost like instagram reels.

Reading isn't one way, the author does not do all of the talking. It's a conversation between you and the author, and you as the reader have to put in just as much work in contributing to this conversation as the author.

How to practically read better

Reading better, or reading intelligently in order to help your understanding of difficult and dense books comes in multiple stages.

The first stage of understanding a difficult book is having what I like to call context. In other words, you should identify the genre of your book, and have a extremely surface level idea on what the book is about.

For instance, taking adler's work as an example, I have identified it as a practical handbook. This handbook is all about how to intelligently stay active in reading, and gain understanding of the ideas that the authors exchange with you.

That's just an example sentence, you can make it as brief or as detailed as you want. However, you should generally keep it as short as possible.

If you find that you cannot get an idea of the book's genre or what it's about, you should do what the Adler calls: "Inspectional reading."

This means taking 1-3 minutes in skimming the author's prologue, the book's blurb, or read some sentences in chapters with no particular order to get a sense of what a book is about. I call this tasting a book.

Now, that you understand what the book is and what its about, you should learn to develop that habit of being a 'demanding reader'.

This means actively asking questions and engaging in the text as you go. This means asking questions like: "What is being said in detail", "Is this true in whole or in part", "So what?"

These may seem like extremely simple questions, and they are if you are reading a simple book. But if you are reading a much more complicated work, answering these questions will greatly improve your understanding in the text, how the author thinks, the argument, etc. Guess what, you'll even be able to discuss with others about the book as well, how cool is that?

A brief example

To put all these steps in practice and to showcase how this is actually helpful, I will pull a random out of context paragraph from Cyclonopedia: Complicity with anonymous materials:

Oil as the post mortem production of organisms is bound to death. Since its ethos, both orgin and end, is purely teleogical, whatever it inspires is founded on death and the logic of death and eventual conclusion. Oil as a hydrocarbon corpse juice is itself a mortal entity, which has been the sources of ideology for petro masonic orders and their policies from OPEC to the agencies of War on Terror to pomo leftists.

You probably have zero idea what you just read, but that's exactly where understanding builds. First, I'll do a brief Inspectional reading, gathering some info about the book and understanding what it is about.

On my copy, a description of the book of the book reads as follows:

"The Middle East is a sentient entity, it is alive!" concludes renegade Iranian archaelogist Dr. Hamid Parsani, before disappearing under mysterious circumstances. The disordered notes he leaves behind testify to an increasingly deranged preoccupation with oil as the lubricant of historical and political narratives."

Pay attention to the usage of Oil here. This paragraph clearly indicates that oil is one of the main subjects of what I am reading. We also know that the book will probably discuss a lot about geopolitics, as well as historical political events which all points to oil in some strange way.

If you read the very first sentence of the paragraph, which states: "The middle east is a sentient entity, it is alive!", I can logically deduce from this that the paragraph we are attempting to understand should probably be taken a little bit more literally.

Notice how a very simple inspectional reading gives context, opens the way for many questions. For instance, if the middle easy is apparently sentient, then is oil itself 'sentient'? Are these two seperate entities, or one?

This is exactly what it means to treat reading as a conversation. It does help to write these questions in the margins as you are reading, Adler calls this "Making the book your own."

So now that we understand that the author is likely being a little literal here in Oil being a mortal entity, if for the middle east being a sentient living entity we still have many questions.

We may not (Or may) understand what "petro masonic orders" are, or what "pomo leftists" are. These are what I like to call 'term knowledge gaps', these require their own strategy of dealing with.

However, we can nevertheless summarize our paragraph into a very brief sentence of our own. Something like: "Oil is a sentient, mortal entity and has been the driving force of many ideologies and policies with a specific agenda."

This sentence isn't perfect. It extracts many of the nuances and details out, however it gives a good brief summary of it's essence, a key to understanding the text. If you can understand the whole picture, you then understand details and how it relates to the picture.

A word of caution..

What I just wrote above is an extremely distilled summary of Adler's guidance and steps in reading difficult books. It goes much deeper than this, and the steps I have outlined apply very differently depending on the particular genre of book you are reading.

These steps also apply differently depending on the genre of book that you are reading. The same steps that are applied to reading a novel, cannot be applied to reading a book of history, vice versa. You should learn to be adaptive as you read, and apply the steps according to what the book demands.

There is a really good quote from Francis Bacon's of studies:

Some books are to be tasted, others to be swallowed, and some few to be chewed and digested: that is, some books are to be read only in parts, others to be read, but not curiously, and some few to be read wholly, and with diligence and attention.

You should never apply the same effort in understanding Harry Potter, like you read a work of shakespeare. If you wish to become a better reader, the great skill you can develop is understanding which book deserves what from you.

If you are serious about intelligent reading, please go get yourself a copy of this wonderful book.
