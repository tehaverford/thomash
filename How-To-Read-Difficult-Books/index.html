How To Read Difficult Books

Thu Oct 29 2026 19:00:00 GMT-0500 (Central Daylight Time)

Last week, I wrote a post on How To Read Better. My primary goal in that post was to provide a gateway to practical guidance on dissecting and actually understanding much more complex and dense works of literature.

The 'gateway' is the popular work by Mortimer J. Adler. If you haven't already, you should check it out, as it is still very relevant to this topic.

However, what I didn't go into in depth was the actual nuance of misunderstanding or failing to understand the text.

More specifically, I could not distinguish between the different types of misunderstandings that arise in readers as they read books. Each type requires its own strategy and system to deal with.

These 'types' or, rather, 'reasons' look like the following:

  • Language Gaps
  • Logic Gaps
  • Knowledge gaps
  • Terminology gaps

Language Gaps

This is probably one of the most common reasons for not being able to understand a text. This usually takes the form of reading a sentence and not being able to extract meaning from it because of the variety of unfamiliar words that are rapidly introduced to us.

This becomes especially common when reading works from the 18th century on a variety of different philosophies.

Unfortunately, a very common habit among inexperienced readers is immediately attempting to look it up in a dictionary or on Google and then rereading the paragraph until they grasp some understanding of the text.

Despite what your public school may teach you, this is a mistake for a couple of reasons. First, this interrupts your train of thought as you read. When engaging with a book that's especially dense, you need your thinking to get into a particular zone to extract meaning most effectively.

Occasionally, this means not being able to understand words and almost feeling as though you aren't grasping anything from the text. Nevertheless, you should keep reading. Instead, you should circle the unfamiliar word (particularly with a pencil if you have a physical copy), then schedule a time to look it up later.

This tells your brain not to panic: you will come back to this later.

Secondly, you have to understand how the specific word works as a whole in the paragraph you are reading. If you immediately stop and look up the word without understanding the whole context, it can be hard to apply your general understanding of the word in a way that helps you better understand what you are reading.

Some words are a little tricky to understand based on a general definition alone. You need the whole paragraph that mentions the word, as well as the book's context, which can serve as an example and help you understand the word beyond a generic Google definition.

Logic Gaps

This usually means being able to understand a paragraph completely, as well as each individual word, but struggling to understand the idea the author is attempting to convey.

This usually happens as a result of the idea being very dense, complicated, and sophisticated. One of the best ways to counter this is by taking the paragraph that confuses you and dissecting it into individual parts. This means separating each argument from the others and ultimately extracting the paragraph's 'skeleton'.

This helps you list each argument, as well as the relationships each argument has with the others. This helps to build a bigger picture and understand the idea as a whole.

To give a quick example, I'll use an out-of-context paragraph from something I've been reading: Cyclonopedia: Complicity with Anonymous Materials, page 12, in the context of the Cross of Akht:

The cross is comprised of two main parts, the star-head and the handles. The basic geometry of the head consists of a decagon with Khur (the Sun.) But one side of the Sun is forked, to form two handles. One can thus surmise that this is no Zurvanite star or Sun because the star is incomplete and lacks the full complement of ten triangles corresponding to the sides of the decagon. The last sacred triangle has been unknown. Recall, however, that they spoke always of a buried terrestrial sun which must be exhumed, a rotting sun oozing black flame, the black corpse of the sun. And even the name Akht corresponds to the incomplete form of the broken star. One must be blind not to notice that even the ancient Persian word Akht or Axt (أخت meaning pest) is a truncated of the ancient word Akhtar (أختر, Star, referring to the Sun) with its last letter (the letter R, ر) cut off.

There are a lot of things going on here. Of course, it's difficult to understand this without some context from the book, which you can read about here, without my having to repeat myself.

Stepping through this passage carefully, it reads like the following:

  • The star has two handles as well as the head
  • Features a decagon corresponding to the sun
  • The star is incomplete
  • Therefore Parsani (the current narrator) concludes that it is no Zurvanite star (which is its polar opposite)
  • The missing triangle has been replaced by handles
  • "Akht" corresponds to the black star
  • "Akht" is a truncated form of Akhtar.

Therefore, based on the name of the figure we are studying (the Cross of Akht) and how much we have distilled this paragraph, it appears that the star is, in itself, a manifestation of incompleteness.

Notice that the summary sentence I just wrote doesn't go over the 'black-flame' and other symbolic concepts; it just gives a very brief description based on everything that we know.

Breaking a paragraph down into various parts is an extremely powerful way to combat confusion when getting into a dense work.

Knowledge Gaps

Sometimes, the book itself may not actually be 'difficult' in the regular sense of the word; maybe you just need some background information on what you are reading. For instance, if you are reading Ulysses by James Joyce, you will require some Irish history to make sense of what is going on.

However, if you do not know of any Irish history, it would be like trying to make out your room and where you are in it while having a blindfold on you.

This can sometimes be a little trickier to deal with than other kinds of gaps we can encounter when reading difficult books.

This is for the simple reason that we can't recognize Irish history when we encounter it. After all, you only know what you know. Especially if it's a novel, it can be hard to distinguish between the fictional setting and the concepts it builds on from the real world.

Nevertheless, what can help is to circle specific characters' names that you suspect may have some historical significance. The book may or may not mention urban legends from a particular period of history.

When I was reading Cyclonopedia, I noticed that it was filled with a lot of Iranian history in the very first chapter. However, I did not know this, so I believed the 'Haftvad family' to be its own set of characters that the author has just dropped into my lap without telling me who they were. This, of course, made my understanding of the book very poor.

What I did, however, was circle those names, like Haftvad and Akht. This way, when I finished reading, I could look up these names and see whether I could find information about them beyond what the book had introduced me to. It turns out that it's genuine Iranian history!

Not only did this dramatically improve how I saw the book and my understanding of the initial text that I read, I got a pretty good story out of it.

In short, anything that you suspect may be a real person mentioned in the book, or a potential urban legend or myth referenced, you should circle and look up. HOWEVER, be careful not to look it up immediately after circling it. Do not break your reading time down into multiple Google sessions.

As a general rule of thumb, I like to look up anything that I'm confused about only after I have read 20 pages, no exceptions.

Terminology gaps

A terminology gap refers to not being able to understand the author's language, so to speak. This can be a problem, especially when you're reading books that deal with technical subjects, but it happens in every genre as well.

This kind of gap falls into the same category as the language gaps I mentioned earlier; however, these cannot be approached in the same way. Of course, some books have a vocabulary section at the very end, where you can get a quick glance at each term and what it means. However, these are meant for refreshing your memory after you have read the entire book and should not be used during your initial reading.

There are multiple reasons for this. One of the main reasons for not understanding a term is that you do not understand the concept that is linked to it. If you did, you would most likely understand the term. Therefore, looking in an index will introduce you to the concept that you did not understand while reading the book.

Even if you did understand the concept, if there were multiple concepts involved and you were not sure which belonged to which term, having its definition supplied by an index will not benefit you if you wish to become skilled in understanding an author and reading difficult books. This is especially relevant since not all difficult books have indexes.

Instead, one of the most effective techniques for combating this is by taking an element of what we did when handling language gaps. Circle the terms you do not understand, and plan to look them up sometime later.

However, when you go back, the steps that you apply to understand these terms will be different. A great way to develop understanding is by capturing the entire paragraph in which the term is used. A paragraph can provide context for a term, offering many clues about what exactly it means.

Sometimes, the term is not immediately clear when it's first introduced. In that case, you should look for other paragraphs in which that exact same term is used and see if its meaning becomes clearer.

Also, pay attention to 'conjunction words.' In other words, pay attention to words that pair concepts with specific terms. You should circle these as well!

More specifically, you should pay attention to transition and linking words (conjunctions and adverbs). Take a look at this guide for helpful examples.

What can also happen when reading difficult books

So, we've gone through four different reasons why you might struggle with reading a difficult book. You may feel that you are equipped to deal with all of these (or maybe not), and are almost motivated to just get on with this post and start reading!

Don't kid yourself just yet; in reality, it's much more complicated than this. There are many more reasons why a difficult book can be so difficult, but I just don't have the time or patience to write another section about them.

The truth is, it can be a mix of different reasons. A difficult book might be hard because it involves both language gaps and dense ideas. If you are really unlucky, it can sometimes even be all of the above.

I also wish to state that if you feel that a book is too difficult for you and you just aren't getting a lot of enjoyment out of it, you are absolutely obligated to drop it. Please do not grit your teeth and force yourself to enjoy it; this associates reading with pain and suffering, something that humans (who are ultimately animals) try their absolute best to avoid, and for good reason.

While reading can sometimes be very difficult, it should ultimately be enjoyable with lots of rewards at the end. It should increase your imagination, your understanding of the world, your understanding of others, etc. There is a reason why we, as people, must be 'well-read'.

On 'speed-reading'

In the age where speed is the most worshipped asset in everything, speed-reading has arisen to apply this to literature. Most 'speed-reading' is either flat-out fake, manipulated, or somewhere in between. Genuine 'speed-reading' gives very diminishing returns, especially when your goal is to learn from books.

This isn't to say that speed-reading is totally fake, but you should absolutely throw it out if you seek to become a reader and read difficult books. As a matter of fact, you should be proud that you read slowly. It's better to read deeply than to be someone who has read vast amounts and yet remains very ignorant.

While I won't say that anybody reads 'too slowly', I will say that people tend to read more slowly than necessary. Your reading speed is not entirely its own skill; it depends on other factors as well.

For instance, how short is an article? How many of its historical references (if any) are you already familiar with? Is it on a topic you already have some knowledge on? All of these are questions that affect how quickly you can get through it. If you can absolutely just race through text and get what you need from it, then perhaps it's a book, article, or blog-post (hopefully, not mine) that only needs a simple skimming.

This is an extremely general rule of thumb, but books that you can absolutely race by and get what you want generally aren't very challenging or even worth reading if you wish to become a more attentive and better reader.

Summary

In short:

  • Language gaps: Circle the word(s) you do not understand, schedule time to look them up later, and keep reading.
  • Logic gaps: Break the paragraph down into multiple arguments instead of one and see how they link to each other.
  • Knowledge gaps: Pay attention to any potential historical and mythical references, highlight them, and look them up later.
  • Terminology gaps: Circle transition words, as well as the paragraph in which the term is used, to understand whether a concept is linked to it. Only look it up as an absolute last resort.

You are now equipped with many strategies for dealing with difficult reading. However, this does not mean that you know how to read deeply. Being able to read difficult books and extract meaning from their text does not mean you can read into this meaning deeply and with attention.

Reading deeply is a separate art and must be practiced on its own terms. If you are interested, please check out my blog post: How-To-Read-Better

On a final note, it should not go unmentioned that reading 'difficult books' does not mean suffering through every reading session. If you are reading a book which tends to feel like this, it is most likely a bad book no matter how overhyped it may seem on social media. You should drop such a work.
